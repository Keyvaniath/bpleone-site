Micron’s Best Quarter Ever Wasn’t Enough

Posted October 19, 2026Author Brandon LeonData as of Friday, October 16 close

The verdict

On September 28 we published a central test, alongside several supporting indicators: a fiscal-Q1 gross-margin guide at or above this quarter's roughly 86% keeps our thesis intact, and a guide below 80% breaks it.[1] Micron guided to 86.25% on a non-GAAP basis and 85.95% on a GAAP basis, alongside the largest quarter in its history: $54.23 billion of revenue, an 87.0% gross margin, and $33.20 billion of adjusted free cash flow.[2] The test passed. The thesis is intact.

The stock is down about 30% from its June high and has given up its trillion-dollar valuation.[6] That is not a contradiction, and this note is mostly about why. We initiate at hold with a $1,090 twelve-to-eighteen-month target, about 24% above Friday’s close of $878.84.

This note is seventeen days late, and we said otherwise in writing. The September 28 preview promised that “the rating and a 12-18 month target come in our update by Friday, October 2.” October 2 passed with nothing published. The test resolved on September 30 and the grade below is unchanged by the delay, because the line was set before the print and the result is a matter of record. But a research note that sets a date and misses it has spent some of the only thing it has. The change we made after the last time this happened was to stop promising publication dates and start grading on resolution instead; that rule was already written down, and this note broke it anyway. Noted here rather than quietly dropped.

The part the grade conceals

Our test defined a pass at roughly 86% and a break below 80%, and said nothing about the six points in between. An 82% or 84% guide was entirely plausible, and it would have returned no verdict at all. That is the same drafting error we identified in public three weeks earlier, when an inflation test printed inside a gap we had left between its own two branches — and we wrote at the time that a test which cannot answer for a plausible outcome is not yet a test. We then wrote another one with a hole in the middle.

For the record, the range we should have published: at or above roughly 86% supports the view; between 80% and roughly 86% requires us to re-examine the earnings estimate and the reason for the compression, without abandoning the thesis; below 80% breaks it. The four tests in section 5 are written so that every outcome lands on one side of a line.

2. The quarter itself was the largest in the company's history

Revenue of $54.23 billion beat the company's own June guide of $50.0 billion by 8.5% and consensus of $51.24 billion by 5.8%. Non-GAAP earnings of $33.42 a share beat the $31.59 consensus. Operating cash flow was $43.97 billion and adjusted free cash flow $33.20 billion in a single quarter.[2] Guidance for the quarter now running calls for $61.5 billion of revenue, 13% above the quarter just reported, and $38.15 of earnings.

3. So why did the stock fall?

Three reasons, and we should be straight that our own August work named two of them as the bear case before any of this happened.

China. ChangXin Memory Technologies announced progress on a denser DRAM design and signalled server-memory products using it by the end of 2026. Citi has said mass production is unlikely before 2028 and Goldman Sachs has called the threat exaggerated, but the market did not wait for the argument to be settled.[5]

The patent case. Netlist's complaints at the International Trade Commission, covering high-bandwidth memory and DDR5 products, add a legal risk that could take years to resolve and carries material downside. There has been no ruling. The investigation was instituted on September 23.[5]

The cycle. The selloff is consistent with growing concern that memory pricing is at or near its peak, and the guided step down in gross margin gave that view its first number from the company itself. Spot DRAM has gone sideways and contract momentum is easing, even as one broker still forecasts double-digit fourth-quarter contract gains.[3][5] Rising rates and a tech tape that shed more than a trillion dollars in value did the rest.[5]

In memory, the stock has historically peaked before the earnings do. In 2018 the share price topped roughly three months before peak quarterly earnings printed, with the trailing multiple near three times. A low forward multiple is not by itself evidence that the shares are cheap, if the earnings in the denominator are near a cyclical peak. Micron topped on June 25.
In plain terms: The company is doing better than expected and the shares are worth less than they were. Investors are not disputing this quarter's profits. They are betting the next ones are smaller, because a Chinese rival says it is catching up, a patent case could block some products, and memory prices rise and fall in long cycles that usually turn while the news is still good.

4. The rating, and why hold

At $878.84 the stock trades on 5.51 times the fiscal-2027 consensus earnings that stood before the print. Our defended range for this business is 5.5 to 7.0 times next-year earnings, which puts fair value at $877 to $1,116 on those estimates. Friday’s close sits about two dollars above the floor of that range. Three weeks ago, on the eve of the print, it traded at $1,082.28 — inside the band, but in its top quarter. Our probability-weighted value is $1,090.

That leaves roughly 24% of upside, which is not obviously a hold. The reason we are not more constructive is the shape of the distribution rather than its midpoint: our own scenario work carries a bear case running to the $500s on a first cut to forward estimates, against a bull case we cannot underwrite until the supply agreements earn credit in a multiple. An upside of 24% against a downside of that size is not the asymmetry that earns a buy, and the two risks now pricing the stock — Chinese supply and the patent case — are precisely the ones that would widen the left tail rather than the right.

That combination argues for patience rather than enthusiasm, for one reason: the risks pricing the stock down are the risks our own work identified in August as the bear case, and they are not yet resolved. We wrote then that the whole question is whether peak earnings are a peak or a plateau, that the thesis breaks on the first cut to fiscal-2028 estimates rather than on any chart level, and that Chinese supply and the Netlist case were the two things that could do damage without a single customer cutting spending. Those are now the headlines. Being early to name a risk is not the same as knowing how it resolves.

Showing one piece of arithmetic we previously asserted. The preview described the valuation as the market “paying for about half of those earnings to last.” That is a claim about normalised earnings, and it deserves its working rather than a reader's trust. Normalising at twelve times mid-cycle earnings, our valuation range for this business across a full cycle, the September 25 price of $1,082.28 implied roughly $90 of permanent annual earnings, or 57% of the fiscal-2027 consensus of $159.49. At Friday’s $878.84 the same arithmetic implies about $73, or 46%. “About half” is the right order of magnitude; the precision belongs to the normalizing multiple, and ours is now on the page instead of behind the sentence.

The price does not require growth. It requires durability. On a ten percent return and a ten times exit multiple, fiscal 2027's earnings need only hold flat for four years — you are even allowed a small annual decline. That is the entire investment case in one sentence.

On why the target is reaffirmed and not raised. A lower share price raises the upside to an unchanged target; it does not by itself raise fair value, and the estimate increase that would is the one item we have not verified since the results. So the target stays where it is until we hold a post-print consensus and can rework the assumptions behind it, rather than nudging a number on the strength of a quarter whose central metric has just stopped rising. The $1,090 is carried forward from work dated August 18, it has survived a round trip from $820 to $1,255 and back to $879, and it will be re-derived in full rather than nudged.

6. What makes this note wrong

We hold Micron, and it is the largest position in the book we write about, so a hold rating on a stock we own and have not sold deserves more skepticism than a rating on something we do not. The target is reaffirmed from work done at a different price with different estimates, which is a defensible choice and also a convenient one. Our defended multiple is a judgment drawn from memory's history of not sustaining peak multiples, and if the long-term supply agreements genuinely broke that cycle, the range is too low and the Street's $1,555 average is nearer the mark than ours. And the test we published measured the company when the thing that moved the stock was the cycle; passing it told us less than we expected it to, which is worth remembering the next time we write one.
