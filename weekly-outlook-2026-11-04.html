Weekly outlook · two weeks to October 30, 2026

The Bill Moved Down the Supply Chain

Two weeks ago the question was whether the cloud buyers would keep spending, and three of the four said more while every proof test we set on their prints passed and the shares were sold on each report anyway. Within three sessions the sellers were the ones raising money, with Marvell paying $115B in its own stock for Lumentum and Oracle selling $10B of shares alongside $10B of bonds. The market’s question has moved from who buys the capacity to who pays for it, and it is now asking the suppliers.

Author Brandon Leon Posted Wednesday, November 4, 2026 Updated Thursday, November 5, 2026, after the close (addendum in section 5) Data Index, yield and prediction-market figures through Tuesday, November 3; Wednesday’s closes, AMD’s report and the election count (as of 9:25 p.m. ET Wednesday) noted where used; Thursday’s closes in the addendum Coverage The two weeks · the scorecard · passed tests, sold shares · where the money is being raised · what lands next

The Demand Case Passed Every Test and the Financing Question Moved Down the Chain

We said two events would settle October, the Federal Reserve’s meeting on the 28th and the first 2027 spending guides from the large cloud buyers, and both landed on the side the demand case needed. The committee held, Alphabet said its capital spending will “increase significantly” next year, Amazon said “meaningfully more,” and Microsoft said its spending accelerates into the first half of its fiscal 2027, which is why the three proof tests we attached to the prints passed.1

That should have ended the argument, yet the Nasdaq fell 2.9% on Friday in its worst session since June and the semiconductor index fell 7% in a day on a PC-chip guide from Intel.2 The ten-year yield closed Friday 38 basis points below its October high, so the rates explanation we ruled out two weeks ago stays ruled out.

What the market graded was the cost of the capacity the buyers had just committed to, as three of the four quarters came with negative or shrinking free cash flow and by Monday the suppliers were the ones issuing stock. That is the financing reading from October 20, moved one step down the supply chain.

1. The two weeks in one picture

The Chip Line Broke on the Strongest Demand Evidence of the Year

From the October 16 close to the October 30 close the Dow fell 0.4%, the S&P 500 1.0%, the Nasdaq 2.7% and the semiconductor ETF 3.2%. The shape matters more than the end points, because the chip index rose 7.7% into the Wednesday close before the first guides and lost 10.5% in the three sessions after them (Chart 1).3 October was the Nasdaq’s worst month since March 2025 at −5.3% while the Dow added 1.7%.2 It is the same dispersion as two weeks ago with one new feature: the chip line broke after a hold from the Fed and on the best demand evidence the buyers have given all year.

Meta would only say it keeps investing aggressively, so we grade to the stricter text. What matters for the argument is that nobody pointed lower, which means the turning branch never fired.1

The December Call Died on Its Own Wording

The Fed test died on the reading it named, falling from 79.5% the evening of the hold to 69.5% two days later and under a line set one point below the pre-meeting low, which left no room for a Friday wobble. It has since gone back to 74.5% (Chart 2). We do not re-grade a dead call on a later print, so the lesson is about where the line was drawn and it is recorded.3 What the round trip shows is that the committee’s December was not what moved the chips, since the hike price recovered on Monday while the chip index fell another 2.7%.

3. Passed tests, sold shares

Five Prints Cleared Their Numbers and Four Were Sold

Set the prints side by side (Chart 3). Meta raised its 2026 spending floor on October 21 and fell 7% the next day, Microsoft beat the Azure line at 48% against 45 and closed flat. Alphabet’s cloud backlog cleared $560B at $561B and the stock had its worst day on record at −12.6%, and Amazon’s AWS grew 39.3% against the 37% test and fell 1.4% in a market down 2.9%. Intel, which had no test because its reaction turns on foundry news with no number attached, guided the fourth quarter to $15 to $16B against a $17.1B consensus and fell 29%.4 AMD cleared its line on Tuesday night, rose 7% in late trading and closed Wednesday up 1.0% at $488.78 while the chip ETF fell 2.7%, the first beat of the season that was not sold, which is why it is the week’s test and not this fortnight’s story.7

What the Four Sold Passes Shared Was the Bill

What the four sold passes had in common was cash, as Alphabet’s free cash flow was −$9B for the quarter and Meta’s free cash flow was −$2.1B. Amazon’s trailing free cash flow is −$42B after it raised $115B of long-term debt this year against $0.7B in the same nine months of 2025, and Microsoft will use outside funding when the terms are attractive.1 After October 28 the question was who writes the checks, and three of the four answers involved somebody other than the company’s own operations.

Intel Is the Same Story From the Other End

Intel’s release blames a PC slowdown it expects to last through the first half of 2027 and cites industry-wide shortages of substrates and memory, the same shortage Micron reported as its best quarter ever.4 That reading is ours, because neither company made it, and part of the PC’s bad quarter is the AI buildout’s bill arriving at a different customer. The market sold the PC guide as a chip-demand signal and took AMD, Qualcomm and Arm down with it.

4. Rates eased, and the companies that need money fell

Rates and Credit Did Not Move

Start with what did not move: the ten-year closed October 30 at 4.93%, down from 5.10% on October 16 and 5.31% at the October 5 high, closed Tuesday at 4.95%, and the investment-grade and high-yield bond funds ended the stretch within 1% of where they began (Chart 4).3 A rates event, or a general credit event, does not take a tenth off the chip index in three sessions while both sit still.

The Group That Has to Raise Money to Build Is the Group That Fell

What moved was the group that has to raise money to build, as CoreWeave’s shares fell 37% in October with its default swaps last quoted on the 27th near 1,040 basis points, between our two lines. A Financial Times report on Friday, relayed by CNBC, said Blackstone had told investors that Anthropic may not get an investment-grade rating on its debut.5 Then on Monday Oracle said it will sell $10B of stock and $10B of bonds for its AI buildout and fell 8%, and Marvell announced an all-stock purchase of Lumentum and fell 13% while Lumentum fell 18%.6 Intel’s release, for its part, lists a $23B public equity offering completed in the quarter.4

The Marvell Terms Say Where the Bill Went

Each Lumentum share becomes 4.5 Marvell shares, which the companies value at $115B of enterprise value, about $1,132 a share and a 23.9% premium to the October 28 close, the last day before the talks leaked, with the close expected in the first half of 2028. Management calls the deal accretive in its first year after closing, but the presentation does not say whether that counts the billion dollars of synergies it promises, and our arithmetic on calendar-aligned numbers puts it about 2% dilutive before them.6 From the unaffected close to Monday Marvell lost 32% and Lumentum 25%, and at Tuesday’s close the ratio valued Lumentum at $774 a share while Lumentum traded at $680, a 12% gap for an eighteen-month wait (Chart 5).3

The Financing Question Has Reached the Sellers’ End of the Chain

A supplier paying for capacity with its own shares while the market marks those shares down is the financing question arriving at the sellers’ end of the chain. On October 20 the stressed balance sheet belonged to a customer, and on November 2 it belonged to Oracle, Intel and Marvell, each of which chose equity. That is the strongest evidence so far that the drawdown is about the cost of capacity and not the demand for it, with one caveat we cannot size, which is that Friday had month-end and election positioning in it too.2

5. What could clarify the outlook

AMD Cleared Both Lines and Was the First Beat Not Sold

AMD reported Tuesday after the close with revenue of $13.4B, up 45%, a record $8.5B in data center, up 97%, a non-GAAP gross margin of 56% with the first Helios racks running at Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and Oracle, and a fourth-quarter guide of about $17B. The bar our coverage named was the $16.14B fourth-quarter consensus, and the line we registered was a gross margin at or above 56% with racks shipping, and both were met. The shares rose about 7% in late trading, gave most of it back overnight as the election result came in, and closed Wednesday up 1.0% at $488.78 on a day the chip ETF fell 2.7%.7 Two beats this year were sold on the day and this one was not, and whether that holds to Friday is the week’s test. The same release shows the supplier side of the pattern, with free cash flow of $554M, down from $1.6B, and total debt of $7.1B against $3.2B in June.

The Sweep Moved the Futures Overnight and the Cash Session Reversed Them

The midterms were Tuesday and the count came in a Democratic sweep, as NBC News projected the House for Democrats at 11:04 p.m. Eastern and the Senate at 11:23 with six seats flipped (Iowa, Kansas, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas) for 51 called against 39 and ten races open. Republicans had gone in holding the chamber 53 to 47, and prediction markets had made Democrats favorites in both chambers. The sell-side framing CNBC carried on Monday was that a sweep was not priced and would weigh on AI shares, and the first answer agreed, with S&P 500 futures down 1.2% and Nasdaq-100 futures down 2.1% at 11:20 p.m. and the ten-year yield slipping to 4.90%. AMD’s late-trading gain shrank to about 3%, which CNBC tied to the sweep and to the Texas governor-elect’s pledge of a data-center moratorium. By 9:25 p.m. Wednesday NBC had the Senate at 54 to 44 with seven seats flipped (Maine added) and two races uncalled, and the House at 242 to 151. The cash session reversed the futures, as the S&P 500 closed up 1.6%, the Dow 3.0% and the Nasdaq 1.4%, while the chip ETF fell 2.7% and the ten-year ended at 4.89%. We have no edge on the count and set no test on it, so the week’s tests stand as written.8

Also this week: Treasury’s refunding details Wednesday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern with $618B of borrowing estimated for the quarter, Coherent and Arm after the close Wednesday, the October jobs report Friday at 8:30 after September’s 29,000, and Vistra’s first 2027 guide on Friday, graded against the $8.1B line in our October note.9

Addendum, Thursday, November 5, after the close. The test did not wait for Friday, as the Nasdaq fell 5.5% into a correction, the S&P 500 4.4% and the Dow 3.4%, and the chip ETF fell 11.2% to 447.73 against the 546.40 line. AMD closed down 17.3% at $404.40, Micron 17.5% and Nvidia 9.3%, and Marvell fell 16.4% to $133.55, which put 4.5 Marvell shares at $601 against Lumentum at $509.70, a gap of 15.2%. The explanation on the tape was the election, with the Democratic sweep read as more regulation of the AI buildout and the state ballot measures against data centers cited, and the companies that rent out capacity fell hardest. The ten-year ended at 4.84%, the high-yield fund was down 0.3% and the VIX closed at 26.6; the Kalshi price of a December increase was 66.5% at 1:58 p.m. against the 69.5% line. On our scorecard the first test is answering itself, toward the de-rating having moved past the numbers and the financing reading holding, with a third candidate cause this note did not weigh, which is policy, and both tests grade against Friday’s closes as written.11

What would change our view — set before the events
  1. AMD and the reaction (Friday’s close): no bottom call until the market buys a beat. AMD cleared its line, so if the semiconductor ETF closes Friday above 546.40, the October 16 weekly close, the market has bought a beat and the rule lifts. At or below 546.40, the de-rating has moved past the numbers and the financing reading holds.
  2. December pricing after the jobs report (Friday’s close): at or above 69.5%, the post-meeting low, the committee’s path survives the labor data. Below it, the data has started to move December. Below 50% is the regime change we named on October 28.
  3. The ten-year (standing): a Friday close at or above 5.31% puts the pressure back. A close back above 5.10%, the October 16 level, after Wednesday’s refunding says the supply is biting without a new high.
  4. The financing names (standing): CoreWeave’s five-year swap below 800 basis points heals, at or above 1,140 deepens. The gap between Lumentum’s price and 4.5 Marvell shares wider than 15% says the market doubts the close; under 8% says it believes it.
What makes this note wrong

Three equity raises in a week is a pattern to us and a coincidence to a skeptic, and the skeptic has a point, since Intel’s offering closed before any of this, Oracle’s need was known, and Marvell’s ratio was set at a price three weeks old. Friday carried month-end and election positioning we cannot separate out. The capex grade is inconclusive on our own scoring, so the demand evidence is three statements of direction without a 2027 number among them, and the author holds several of the names that fell, Marvell among them, which is a reason to check this reading harder and not a reason to trust it.

What it means: The big cloud companies said they will spend more next year and their shares fell anyway, because the spending is being paid for with borrowed money and new shares, and this week the chip suppliers started doing the same. AMD’s good report was the first one the market did not sell, and whether that holds to Friday, and whether the Fed’s December increase survives the jobs number, are the two tests.

6. Something to think about

AOL Paid in Paper at the Top

On January 10, 2000, America Online agreed to buy Time Warner for about $165B in AOL shares, the largest merger ever at the time, in which the acquirer had the expensive stock and the target had the cable systems, the magazines and the film studio. The structure was all paper, with AOL holders keeping 55% of the result, and the Nasdaq peaked two months later.10

It is remembered as the worst deal in corporate history, which is near the lesson without being it, because the lesson is what an all-stock offer says about the buyer’s view of its own currency. A board that pays in shares would sooner hand you its stock than its cash, and the market reads that message whether or not the board means to send it. Marvell fixed its ratio at a price from October 28, and the market has since marked that currency down by a third.

Which leaves the question for every all-stock deal struck inside a de-rating: is the buyer’s stock the cheapest thing it can pay with, or the most expensive thing it owns?
