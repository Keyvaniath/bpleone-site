Sold Out Through 2028

ASML beat the top of its own margin guidance, raised the full year again, and said plainly that it will not be able to build enough machines to meet demand in either 2027 or 2028. The shares fell, and that decision is now ours to make rather than describe.

Author Brandon Leon Posted Monday, October 19, 2026 Data Q3 results of October 14; price October 19 intraday Rating Accumulate, unchanged since the July 15 initiation · target about $2,000

On October 14 ASML reported third-quarter net sales of €11.66 billion and a gross margin of 57.5% — above the top of its own 55–57% guidance. It raised full-year sales to €44–45 billion and guided the December quarter to €14.2–15.2 billion, which would be the largest quarter in the company’s history. Then it said the thing that matters more than any of those numbers: its planned supply will not fully meet customer demand in 2027 or in 2028, and it is investigating capacity increases for 2028 beyond the 30% it has already committed to.1

The stock fell on the report and has kept falling. It trades near $1,647, roughly 8% below where it sat when we last published work on it.3

That matters more than usual, because in July’s initiation we wrote a specific sentence: we would add on a semiconductor-wide pullback into $1,550–$1,650, and we would not chase. The stock is in that range today. A published condition that goes live stops being analysis and becomes a decision, and this desk has a poor recent record on exactly that transition.

1. What the quarter actually said

Three things, in descending order of durability.

The margin cleared its own ceiling. 57.5% against a 55–57% guide, up from 54.0% in the June quarter. Installed-base management revenue — service and upgrades on machines already in the field, the most predictable line in the business — came in at €3.3 billion, about €400 million above guidance.

The year went up again. Full-year sales of €44–45 billion, narrowed upward from €43–45 billion, with a fourth-quarter guide of €14.2–15.2 billion. Systems shipped in the quarter: 121, of which 113 were new.

And the constraint moved from demand to supply. Management said planned capacity will not fully meet demand across both 2027 and 2028. For a company whose entire investment case rests on being the only supplier of extreme-ultraviolet lithography, a stated multi-year shortfall is the most favourable sentence it can utter about pricing.

What it means: ASML makes the machines that print the smallest features on advanced chips, and for the most advanced step it is the only supplier on earth. It just earned a higher profit margin than it told investors to expect, raised its forecast for the year, and said it will not be able to build enough machines to satisfy customers for the next two years. Normally that combination is what a shareholder hopes to read.

2. The shares went the other way

They did not go the other way because of anything in the release. The week that followed was the worst for the Nasdaq since June, driven by a repricing of the credit financing artificial-intelligence build-outs rather than by any change in orders — a subject we take up separately in this week’s outlook. ASML is among the most rate-sensitive assets in that complex: a monopoly whose value sits in cash flows a decade out, discounted at a long bond that touched a cycle high of 5.31% on October 5.

So the stock is down for reasons that have little to do with lithography and much to do with the discount rate and the mood around anything AI-adjacent. That is precisely the circumstance our July note described when it set an add range.

3. The decision, and our record on these

Reaffirming accumulate at a target of about $2,000 is the easy half. The hard half is what this desk does when a published condition goes live, and the honest answer is that we have recently done nothing.

In August a buy gate opened on a power utility we had researched, published and authorised. No order was placed. We reported that failure in three consecutive notes before retiring the framework rather than publishing the same unexercised authorisation a fourth time. A partial-trim order on another holding has now gone unplaced across three notes as well. A range that only ever gets described is not a rule; it is a preference with better formatting.

What we are committing to here

The add range stands as published: $1,550–$1,650, on a semiconductor-wide pullback, without chasing. The stock is at the upper edge of it now.

Under our own trading policy we cannot act on a covered name inside the window around publishing a note on it, so no order from this desk can be placed for five days after this note appears. That is a constraint we wrote for ourselves to stop research and trading from contaminating each other, and it cuts against us here.

What we will do is report the outcome either way in the next note: whether an order was entered, at what level, and if nothing was done, that too. The number above is not the test. Whether it gets executed is.

What makes this note wrong

An add range set in July was set at a different long-bond yield, and a monopoly is still a long-duration asset being repriced by rates rather than by its order book — the range may simply be too high for the regime we are now in. The quarter also flattered itself: about €500 million of net income came from revaluing a venture stake, not from selling machines. “Supply will not meet demand” is a statement about intentions two years out from a company with every incentive to make it, and the one hard number we do not have is bookings, which ASML stopped disclosing. And the author already owns this name, which makes reaffirming a positive rating the comfortable conclusion rather than the brave one.

What it means: The company did better than it promised and told investors it cannot build fast enough to keep up for two more years. The shares fell anyway, because higher interest rates reduce what investors will pay today for profits arriving years from now. That has pushed the price into a range where we said in July we would buy more. We are restating that, noting we cannot act for five days under our own rules, and promising to report what actually happens rather than only what we think.
