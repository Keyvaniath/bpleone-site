Deployment series · No. 1 · the framework · July 31, 2026

Where the next dollar goes. And why it hasn't gone yet.

Author Brandon Leon Posted 2026-07-31 (figures as of the July 30 close) Coverage Deployment · the correlation budget · power · big tech · the cash hurdle

TL;DR: The Micron exit returned the largest cash position this portfolio has carried since coverage began in May — roughly a quarter of the book — and the temptation is to spend it fast. We're not going to, and this note explains the machine that decides when we do. The hurdle is real: six-month bills pay just above 4% while the equity market's cushion over Treasuries sits near zero. The 30-year just closed above 5.2% for the first time since 2007, and the Fed produced three dissents for a hike. The framework is a correlation budget — every candidate is scored on how little it moves with what we already own — plus the axis this earnings week just handed us: the market pays self-funded AI capex and discounts externally-funded AI capex. Five lanes surveyed inside: power (the lead candidate, with an honest new finding about which name actually diversifies), Alphabet (wait-for-price, with an alert level), the memory basket we already hold (thesis survives China's arrival — with named tripwires), two software cases that inverted, and cash itself. Every lane gets a dated gate. Nothing moves before its gate.
The setup

Twelve weeks of Micron coverage ended last night with a broker stop doing what we once failed to do ourselves, and the ledger closed at roughly +53% — all realized, all cash. That cash now sits next to a book that is, by design and by confession, one bet expressed several ways: a memory basket, a lithography toolmaker, and a venture-sized quantum sleeve, every one of them a supplier to the same AI buildout. The exit didn't just return capital. It returned a question we've spent a month researching and never once answering with money: where does the next dollar go, when everything you own already points the same direction?

This note opens the deployment series that answers it — in public, with the same discipline the Micron saga beat into us. One underwrite per note. Falsifiable theses, named kill-shots, pre-committed levels. And a rule we're adopting from the start: the research decides the list; the calendar decides the timing. We've already watched this cash's first temptation come and go inside a single week — the power names fell double digits into the Fed shock and bounced 4% the next day; a software name touched our old buy level for two sessions and then ripped 21% away. Chasing either would have been a mood. The gates below are the alternative to moods.1

1. The hurdle: cash is a position now

Every deployment argument starts with what doing nothing pays, and right now doing nothing pays more than it has in the life of this book. Six-month Treasury bills yield just above 4% — and the bill curve's own upward tilt says the market expects the Fed's next move to be a hike, not a cut. September hike odds sit near 60% after Wednesday's 9–3 hold with Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan all dissenting for a quarter point. The 30-year closed at 5.20%, its first close above that level since July 2007, after twelve straight sessions above 5%.1

Against that, the S&P 500 trades near 21 times forward earnings — an earnings yield in the high fours — which puts the classic equity risk premium at roughly zero: stocks, in aggregate, no longer pay you a cushion for equity risk over what the Treasury pays you for none. That is not a forecast of a crash; markets have run on thin cushions before. It is a statement about the price of impatience. Every dollar we deploy has to clear a real 4% and justify equity risk on top of it in a regime where the long bond is competing for capital — and where the supply of long-dated paper keeps growing: Treasury borrows roughly $671 billion this quarter, and the hyperscalers themselves have become long-end issuers, with the big AI spenders carrying a combined half-trillion dollars of bonds outstanding. The AI buildout is now competing with the Treasury market for the same duration dollars, including ours.1

So the first deployment decision is already made, and it's the least glamorous one in this note: the un-deployed balance goes to work in bills, not in a money-market afterthought. Four percent riskless is the bar every idea below gets measured against — and per the discipline published in this week's outlook, at least half the cash doesn't move at all until the August 12 CPI, the first inflation print carrying the war's oil premium, tells us which Fed we're dealing with in September.

Simply: Safe government bills now pay about 4%, and stocks as a whole are priced so richly that they offer almost no extra reward for taking equity risk instead. That means sitting in cash isn't laziness — it's a real investment position with a real return. Anything we buy has to beat it convincingly. Half the cash stays parked until mid-August inflation data regardless.

2. The framework: a correlation budget and a new axis

This portfolio's problem was never a shortage of ideas. It was that every idea we love is secretly the same idea. The published book — the DRAM basket, ASML, the quantum sleeve — is a supplier-side bet on hyperscaler AI capex, and the July tape kept demonstrating that when that one factor sneezes, everything we own catches it together. So the mandate for new money is scored on one feature above all: correlation to what we already hold, where lower is the thing we're buying. A cheap stock that falls on the same days as our book isn't diversification at any price; it's the same bet wearing a different ticker. We call this the correlation budget, and this week — usefully, brutally — the market ran the experiment for us.

On Wednesday July 29, the Fed held with three hawkish dissents and the 30-year closed above 5.2% for the first time in nineteen years. That session was a stress test no backtest could improve on: it told us, name by name, who actually trades on something other than our factor. Salesforce rose 3.8% through the shock. Constellation Energy barely moved. Vistra fell almost 4% — on top of an 11% bleed the three sessions prior. Synopsys, supposedly a defensive toll-booth, fell 2.6%, right with the semis. One afternoon graded a month of our candidate list.2

The second half of the framework arrived the same week, from the capex prints themselves. We wrote on Sunday that the market now pays capex with proof attached and sells capex on faith. Microsoft and Amazon then refined the axis for us, because Amazon raised its 2026 capex guide to roughly $220 billion — the biggest number of the five — and was paid 9% after hours for it, while Alphabet's raise to $195–205 billion got sold for a week. The difference isn't the size of the check. It's who writes it. Microsoft and Amazon fund the buildout from their own cash flow; Alphabet just executed its first major equity raise in more than twenty years, paused buybacks, roughly doubled its debt inside six months, and will start selling $40 billion of stock at-the-market this quarter. The market's rule, restated: self-funded capex plus monetization gets paid; externally-funded capex gets discounted — whatever the revenue proof looks like. And note what the market forgave: Amazon's actual Q3 revenue guide came in below consensus, and the stock ripped anyway — AWS reaccelerating outweighed a soft guide, because the funding is internal. That axis matters to every lane below, because it tells us which customers of our supplier book are load-bearing and which are leaning on the capital markets in a year when the capital markets have a 5.2% alternative.3

One footnote on earnings quality that belongs in the open, because it now spans two of the five: the AI boom has begun marking itself to market through megacap income statements. Alphabet's headline quarter carried a ~$99 billion unrealized gain on equity stakes, chiefly SpaceX; Amazon's carried a $53.4 billion non-operating gain, primarily its Anthropic investment — nearly two-thirds of its reported net income. Both core quarters were strong on their own operating lines; neither headline EPS number means what a screen says it means. When private AI valuations flow through public earnings at this scale, trailing multiples across the complex quietly stop being comparable — one more reason this series underwrites operating lines and cash flows, not headline prints.3

Simply: Two rules govern every purchase in this series. First: new money must buy things that don't move together with what we already own — and this week's Fed shock conveniently showed us exactly which candidates pass that test. Second: among the AI giants, the market now rewards companies that pay for their AI buildout from their own profits (Microsoft, Amazon) and punishes the one borrowing and selling stock to do it (Google). We'll use both rules on every idea.

3. The five lanes, surveyed honestly

Lane one: power — the lead candidate, with a finding we didn't expect

The cleanest supplier trade left in the AI complex may not be silicon at all. Every dollar of the roughly $700-billion-plus the big four will spend this year needs electrons, and the grid is the one bottleneck China cannot flood: nobody imports American grid capacity. The physical scarcity is not a thesis, it's a filing — PJM's latest capacity auction cleared at its price cap for the fourth straight year, procured $16.4 billion of capacity, attracted just 525 megawatts of new generation, and left the entire footprint 6,831 megawatts short of its reliability requirement for the second consecutive year. Meanwhile the two names we've tracked all summer — Vistra and Constellation — sit 32% and 36% below their 52-week highs, both reporting next week.4

Here's the honest finding, and it cost us a week of watching to learn it: the cheap one and the diversifying one are not the same stock. Vistra — the value name, roughly 16 times forward earnings, consensus targets some 50% above the price — fell 15.5% across the four sessions into the Fed shock. It is AI-demand beta plus rate beta: the very things our book already suffers from, with a new one added. Constellation — the premium nuclear operator at roughly 23 times — fell 6.4% over the same stretch and less than one percent on the shock day itself. On the correlation budget, the expensive stock is the cheaper diversifier. Two caveats keep this from being a table-pounding setup: the capacity-price lever is politically capped (the auction cap itself fell 2.5%, by design), so the bull case runs through energy margins and long-dated datacenter contracts rather than auction prints; and both names bounced only when Microsoft's print lit the AI tape — they are lower-correlation, not un-correlated. The gate, in one sentence: Constellation reports Thursday August 6 before the open and Vistra Friday August 7; if guidance holds and prices sit at or below this week's levels after the prints, Constellation buys first and Vistra stages behind it, with full size waiting for the August 12 CPI. If a hot CPI puts September firmly in play and the prints merely hold, we pass: the book already owns this macro bet three ways.4

Lane two: Alphabet — the right company at the wrong price, with an alert set

Alphabet is the most interesting argument in the market right now: Cloud grew 82% with a $514 billion signed backlog growing $50 billion a quarter, which is the best capex-to-revenue proof any hyperscaler has printed — and the stock still can't get paid for it, because of how the buildout is funded. The trailing valuation that screens cheap is an illusion: last quarter's headline earnings carried a $99 billion unrealized mark on equity stakes — chiefly SpaceX, marked up on its June IPO — worth $6.26 of the $9.11 in reported EPS. Strip it and the core quarter was simply in line, leaving the stock at roughly 25 times forward earnings: a premium to Microsoft's 20, for the messiest capital story in the group — $811 billion of purchase commitments (up half a trillion dollars in a single quarter), a $40 billion at-the-market program that begins selling into every rally this quarter, and management's own promise that 2027 spending rises “significantly” again. We are not sellers of the business; we are refusers of the price. The alert sits near $318 per share — the post-earnings low, where the discount to Microsoft is restored — and the decision print is October 27. Until one of those arrives, Alphabet is a watch, not a buy. It would also flunk the correlation budget anyway: it's the payer side of the exact factor our suppliers monetize.3

Lane three: the memory basket we already own — held, and now properly underwritten

The scariest position in the book after the CXMT shock was the DRAM basket, and we owed it a real underwrite rather than a hope. Here's the verdict: the thesis survives, because China's champion is fenced out of the exact segment where the shortage lives. CXMT's own IPO prospectus contains no HBM project. Its HBM3 remains sampling-only — one research house estimates yields near 25% — with mass production slipping, no EUV access, and U.S. controls that specifically choke the tooling HBM requires. Its commodity DDR5 bits absorb domestically, and — the tell that matters most — its server memory reportedly prices above Samsung's in China: a price-taker sheltering under the shortage umbrella, not an undercutter collapsing it. That is precisely how Samsung can report a record quarter and say, the same day, that the shortage gets more severe in 2027 and runs through 2028 while locking 60–70% of capacity into five-year contracts. And last night the demand side testified in person. On his final earnings call as Apple's CEO, Tim Cook described memory costs as rising at a pace he called a “100-year flood,” pressuring Apple's margins into the September quarter. That is the world's most capital-light hyperscaler confirming the shortage from the buyer's side of the table — in precisely the advanced segments where China's entrant cannot yet compete. The basket's drawdown is the market repricing a 2028 tail, not the next four quarters. We hold — and we publish the tripwires so you can hold us to them:

  • The early warning: CXMT-versus-Samsung server-DRAM pricing in China's spot market flipping from a premium to a discount.
  • The kill headline: verified volume HBM3 shipments to Huawei at workable yields.

Either one reopens this thesis in public.5

Lane four: the two software cases that traded places

A month ago our software shortlist was Synopsys (the defensive anchor) and Salesforce (the contrarian). July inverted them. Synopsys is now the cheap one — 43% off its high, twelve percent below the entry band we once set — and disqualified anyway, twice over: it spent the month trading like a high-beta semi (falling with our book on every bad day, including 2.6% on decision day), and it acquired a genuine moat question when a Chinese AI lab demonstrated a chip designed end-to-end on open-source design tools, a scare that hit its rival Cadence the same day. Management's answer — agentic-design partnerships with Intel, Microsoft, and Nvidia announced last week — is the right answer; whether it's sufficient is an August 26 earnings and September investor-day question, not a today question. Salesforce ran the opposite path: it touched our old band for barely two sessions in late July, V-bottomed 21% on a $1.6 billion federal contract and Agentforce momentum — and, decisively for this framework, rose through the Fed shock while everything AI-adjacent fell. It is the one candidate that has now demonstrated the low correlation against our current book that we're shopping for — at a price 13% above where we'd pay for it. The agents-versus-seats bear case gets adjudicated September 2, when contracted revenue growth either holds its low-teens rate or doesn't. A retest of the low $160s before then, and the band discipline buys it; otherwise we wait for the print.6

Lane five: cash — already deployed, by definition

Covered in Section 1, listed here for completeness because the framework demands it: bills above 4% are a lane, not a waiting room. The balance that hasn't cleared a gate earns the hurdle rate while the calendar works. In a market with no equity cushion, patience is currently the highest-conviction position we own.

Simply: Five options for the money. Power companies (the favorite — AI needs electricity and the grid is genuinely short, but we learned this week the cheap one moves with everything we own, so the steadier one buys first, after next week's earnings). Google (great business, wrong price, funding itself in ways the market rightly distrusts — we have an alarm set lower). Our existing memory fund (we checked the China threat properly: it can't yet make the advanced chips where the shortage is — we hold). Two software stocks that swapped roles (the cheap one stopped diversifying; the diversifying one got expensive). And cash itself at 4%, which everything else has to beat.

4. The gates: the calendar decides, not the mood

Every lane above terminates in a dated event with a pre-committed response. This is the whole lesson of the Micron ledger, applied to buying instead of selling: the plan is written before the tape moves, and the tape is only allowed to answer questions we asked in advance.

What makes this framework wrong

Patience is also a position, and it can also lose. Three ways this note ages badly:

(1) The melt-up resumes without us — if the capex verdict plus a cool CPI reignites the AI tape, our gates will have kept us in bills at 4% while the lanes we correctly identified run 20% without us. Gated discipline caps upside; that's its price, and we're paying it knowingly. (2) The correlation evidence is one week old — Salesforce's shock-day strength and Constellation's calm are single-week findings that a different macro tape could reverse; we're treating them as evidence, not law. (3) The hurdle cuts both ways — if the Fed's hawkish turn breaks something and yields fall hard, cash's 4% evaporates and the duration-sensitive names we're being patient with will have re-rated before our gates open. A wire is a response plan, not immunity — and that includes the wires we've aimed at ourselves.

What this series will deliver

One underwrite per note, in the order the calendar makes them decision-ready: the power pair first (post-print, next week), then whichever of Alphabet, Salesforce, or the memory-tripwire review the gates summon. Each note ships with the same equipment the Micron coverage earned its scars proving out: a falsifiable thesis, named kill-shots, pre-committed entry and exit levels — and, where discretion has ever failed us before, a standing order instead of a promise. The ledger gets graded in the weeklies, as always. We are okay with being wrong. We are not okay with being vague.

Simply: Every possible purchase now has a specific date and a specific test. Earnings next week decide the power stocks. Mid-August inflation decides how much gets spent at all. September and October events decide the software names and Google. Until a test is passed, the money waits in 4% bills. We wrote the plan down before the market could tempt us out of it — because the one time we relied on willpower instead of rules, it cost us, and we published the receipts.
