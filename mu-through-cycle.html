Coverage · Memory / semis · Long

MU — Through-cycle long.

Ticker NASDAQ: MU Sector Memory / semiconductors Posted 2026-05-07 Author Brandon Leon

Thesis in one paragraph

Memory is cyclical. Everyone knows this — it’s the reason these stocks trade at single-digit P/Es even when current margins look incredible. So why, in the middle of the highest-margin quarter in its history, is Micron signing five-year customer agreements?

HBM has quietly changed what “cyclical” means for this company, and the market hasn’t repriced for it. At $668, the price implicitly requires through-cycle EBIT margins of about 38% — below what SK hynix earned last year, with a smaller HBM mix than Micron has today. The gap between that 38% and what I think the right number is (closer to 42%) is the trade. Mid-cycle Micron is worth $750–$850. The single thing that would make me say I’m wrong is HBM revenue mix going flat for two consecutive quarters — I’ll explain why at the end.

1. The business and where we are in the cycle

Start with the business as it actually exists, not as the textbook describes it. Micron is roughly 78% DRAM and 22% NAND, which is the textbook line. The textbook is missing the interesting part: HBM — the high-bandwidth memory that feeds AI chips — is on track to grow from about 6% of Micron’s total revenue in FY24 to nearly 28% by FY27. That isn’t a mix shift. That’s a different business inside the same company.

Conventional DRAM is the cyclical commodity everyone associates with memory. Spot-priced. Three players competing on cost per bit. Margins swing from negative to positive sixty-something depending on which side of the supply-demand line you’re standing on. HBM is none of those things. It’s sole-sourced or dual-sourced per chip platform, contracted under multi-year fixed-price/fixed-volume LTAs, and built to order. Same company sells both. Different worlds.

That decoupling is why I’m writing this piece. Almost every published note I’ve read on Micron is implicitly valuing it as a pure conventional-DRAM cycle stock — the way you’d have valued it in 2018, when HBM didn’t matter and AI was something Andrew Ng tweeted about. That framing was right then. It’s not right now.

Why memory is a through-cycle game, not a quarterly one

Memory has run four full peak-to-peak cycles since 2010. Pick any single quarter and you’ll get a wildly different read on the business depending on where you happen to be standing. The 2018 peak said memory was a printing press; the 2023 trough said it was hopeless; the truth was always somewhere in the middle. The only honest way to value memory is on through-cycle earnings — the average over a full peak-to-peak window, not whichever endpoint flatters your priors.

For Micron from FY11 to FY24, that average was roughly 28% EBIT margin. If you take that number, multiply it by sales, and capitalize at a memory multiple, you get the textbook valuation. It’s defensible if you think the next decade is going to look like the last one.

I don’t, and the reason is gross margins. HBM gross margins run somewhere in the high 50s; conventional DRAM lands in the mid-40s mid-cycle. That spread is real, persistent, and structural — HBM is harder to make, sole-sourced under contract, and the customer pays for the certainty. As HBM grows from 6% to 28% of mix, the blended margin uplift is roughly 12 points at the gross line, which translates to about 14 points at EBIT once you allocate corporate overhead. So the through-cycle EBIT margin number that mattered for the last decade — 28% — should be more like 42% for the next one.

That’s the whole pivot. The cycle’s shape doesn’t change. The floor and the ceiling do. Everything else in this piece follows from that.

2. Model output

So what does the model spit out at 42% mid-cycle EBIT margin? FY27E revenue lands at $118 billion. Peak EBIT margin (cycle peak, not mid-cycle) is 62%. Diluted EPS comes in at $74.50. Free cash flow is $36 billion — enough to fund the $20-billion-and-rising capex program with room left over for buybacks.

The DCF, with a 10% WACC, 2.5% terminal growth, and a 9× exit EBITDA, prices the equity at $925 per share. A 12× multiple on FY27E EPS gets you $895. Sum-of-parts — HBM at 14×, DRAM at 8×, NAND at 6× — lands at $850. Three different methodologies, three different anchors, and they all converge in the same neighborhood.

That convergence is half the reason I’m comfortable here. The other half is that current $668 sits below all three by roughly 30%, which is a bigger margin of safety than I usually get on a high-conviction long.

Key assumptions

Five inputs do most of the work. None of them depends on the world being kind to me.

Mid-cycle DRAM ASP at $0.07 per gigabit — the midpoint of the FY11–FY24 cycle range. This is the assumption a bear should attack first, and it’s the most-conservative way to frame it: I’m saying the next decade looks like the last one in conventional DRAM despite the LTA structural shift.

Mid-cycle EBIT margin at 42%. Historical 28% plus 14 points of HBM mix uplift. The 14 points is the load-bearing assumption in the whole model — if it’s wrong, this thesis falls. I think it’s right and I think the way to prove it is to watch HBM revenue mix every quarter (more on that later).

HBM mix at 25–30% through the cycle. Even at peak, HBM doesn’t cannibalize conventional DRAM — both grow.

Capex at 18–20% of sales mid-cycle, 24–26% at peak as the Idaho fab pulls in. I do not underwrite a permanent 25%+ capex regime; if that becomes the steady state, it’s a yellow flag that supply discipline has broken down somewhere.

Working capital at 12–14% of sales. Memory carries inventory cyclically — build into the trough, work down into the peak. HBM is built-to-order so the swing dampens modestly, but the rhythm is the same.

The single biggest sensitivity in the whole model is the EBIT margin. A 4-point miss (38% instead of 42%) drops the implied price by roughly $145, which is most of the gap to today’s $668. A 4-point beat takes it past $1,000. The whole pitch hinges on that number.

3. Scenarios and reverse-DCF

Every long needs an honest scenario table. Here’s mine.

Bear (20% probability, $475 PT, −29%). HBM commoditizes faster than I expect. CXMT closes the gap. Samsung qualifies HBM4 a quarter early and takes some Vera Rubin volume. Through-cycle EBIT margin compresses to 32%. FY27E EPS lands at $52. Apply a 9× cyclical-trough multiple. Notice that the bear requires three independent things to all happen at once — that’s why I assign it 20%, not 35%.

Base (55%, $895 PT, +34%). HBM4 ships to plan, CY26 capacity sells through, conventional DRAM stays tight through FY27 because the AI buildout keeps diverting wafer capacity that would otherwise serve consumer electronics. Through-cycle EBIT margin lands at 42%. 12× on $74.50.

Bull (25%, $1,150 PT, +72%). The LTAs extend to 2028 and beyond. HBM4E pulls in by six months. Samsung’s HBM4 qualification slips. Micron gets an investment-grade upgrade and the WACC compresses materially. 14× on $82.

Probability-weight all three and you get $865 with a reward-to-risk ratio of 2.6:1. Those numbers describe a position I’m willing to hold size in.

Now flip the model. Hold WACC, terminal growth, and exit multiple constant. Solve backwards: what does today’s $668 price require the market to believe about through-cycle EBIT margin? The answer is approximately 38%.

Pause on that. SK hynix, with a smaller HBM mix than Micron has today, earned 31% in FY24. The market is implicitly telling you that Micron — the company with the second-largest HBM share in the industry — will earn 7 points of through-cycle EBIT margin uplift over a smaller-HBM peer, despite a structurally higher mix and a binding LTA framework that makes its highest-margin segment less cyclical than anything Korea is shipping.

That’s a coherent position only if you simultaneously believe Micron earns less margin per dollar of HBM than SK does, AND that HBM’s gross-margin spread over conventional DRAM is overstated, AND that the cycle is about to mean-revert below the FY11–FY24 historical average. Each of those is a defensible bear argument on its own. Holding all three at once requires a degree of pessimism about memory’s structural position that I haven’t seen anyone defend with numbers. The gap between 38% and 42% is the whole trade.

4. Three things that would change my mind

The honest version of the bear case. If any of these turn up in the data, the thesis is wrong and I'll say so on the next update.

  1. HBM revenue mix flat or declining for two consecutive quarters. Micron discloses HBM revenue as a percentage of DRAM each quarter. Base case has HBM mix climbing from ~12% (FQ2'26) to ~25% (FQ4'26) to ~30% (FQ4'27). If the mix flattens for two quarters, it means either (a) Micron is losing HBM share at NVIDIA (most likely Samsung qualifying and taking volume), (b) yield issues are constraining shipments at the same time conventional DRAM is selling well, or (c) HBM ASP is collapsing faster than units are growing. Any of those breaks the margin uplift assumption that drives 14 points of through-cycle EBIT margin. Track it on every earnings call.
  2. CXMT announces credible HBM3 sample shipping with a hyperscaler customer. CXMT is currently ~3 generations behind (HBM2-class), and US export controls limit their access to advanced lithography (no EUV, capped at SMIC-class nodes). The base case assumes CXMT gets to HBM3-class around 2028 and HBM4-class around 2030 — meaningful but distant. If CXMT announces an HBM3 sample with a Tier-2 hyperscaler customer (e.g., a Chinese cloud) before mid-2027, the catch-up curve is faster than modeled and the moat erodes. The HBM TAM stays the same; Micron's slice gets smaller.
  3. Hyperscaler 2H'26 capex guides down by >10% YoY. The four largest hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon) account for an estimated 45% of FY27E HBM end-demand. The 5-year LTAs lock in 2026 and most of 2027 volume contractually, but the renewal economics in 2028+ depend on hyperscaler willingness to keep capex flat or growing. If 2H'26 capex guides plot a >10% YoY cut on any of those four, it means AI capex digestion is real and the next round of HBM contracts gets renegotiated lower. The LTAs don't break; the next-cycle pricing does.

5. Comp set and peer math

The comp set is small and explicit. Five names plus a benchmark line, and a deliberate exclusion list at the end.

SK hynix is the closest direct comp — biggest HBM player by share, similar customer base, similar cycle position. FY27E P/E around 5.5×, trading at a discount to MU on earnings and parity on book multiples.

Samsung’s memory segment is the second peer, with the caveat that isolating it from the conglomerate requires assumptions. Implied memory-only FY27E P/E is roughly 6.0×.

Western Digital / SanDisk is in despite being NAND-only. The reason is the contrast: back WDC’s multiple out of MU’s multiple, and the residual is HBM-attributable. That residual is what supports the rerate thesis.

Lam Research and Applied Materials are equipment names. They give the second-derivative read-through on memory capex discipline — if Big Three capex stays robust, supply discipline is intact. They trade at a premium (FY27E P/E 16–17×) because they’re not memory cyclicals; they’re capex cyclicals on a different rhythm.

Drop the equipment names from the median calculation (different cycles, different multiples) and the memory-only peer median lands at roughly 6.5× FY27E P/E. Micron at 7× trades at a 1-turn premium to that median. A 12× target multiple represents a 5.5-turn expansion to fair value.

That’s the whole valuation argument compressed into one sentence: anyone making the case that Micron is over-valued at 12× FY27E EPS has to explain why the memory company with the highest HBM mix in the comp set should trade at a discount to a NAND-only pure play.

Why these peers and not others

Quick defense of the inclusions and exclusions, because the rigor of the comp set is half the argument.

Intel is out. They sold their memory business to SK in 2020 and today they’re a logic/fabs company — different cycle, different multiples. Including them inflates the median in a way that isn’t honest.

Lam and Applied Materials are in because the equipment names give the earliest signal on capex discipline. If their memory-segment guidance softens, that’s the leading indicator that the cycle is rolling — earlier than the memory companies will admit it themselves.

TSMC and ASML are out because they’re not memory cyclicals; including them inflates the median and biases the argument upward. Marvell and Broadcom are out for the same reason — HBM-adjacent ASIC players are interesting reads, but they’re not memory peers.

The set is small because the argument is specific. I’m not running a peer screen looking for the most flattering median; I’m checking whether the multiple I’m asking for is defensible against the right comp universe. It is. That’s the end of the valuation case.
