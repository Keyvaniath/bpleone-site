Coverage · AI infrastructure · Alphabet Q3 2026 · Hold, probability-weighted value about $280

The Backlog Cleared. The Cash Did Not.

Posted November 3, 2026Author Brandon LeonData as of Monday, November 2 close

The verdict

Alphabet’s third quarter passed two of the three tests we set in August. Google Cloud revenue grew 103% to $30.7 billion, and the Cloud backlog reached $560.8 billion, above our $560 billion line by eight hundred million.[1][2] The third test was the one the thesis rested on, and it failed: free cash flow was negative $9.1 billion for a second straight quarter, buybacks were zero for a third, and in nine months the company raised $138 billion from debt and equity markets while returning $8 billion.[1][2]

Management held 2026 capital spending at $195–205 billion and said 2027 would “increase significantly,” with the figure to come in February.[3] Our August framework treated that path as the bear case, weighted 25%, with a floor at $290. The shares went through the floor in one session. The funding turn is now our base case. We take the Street’s 2027 earnings whole and pay 18–20 times for them while the spending is financed externally, against 26–28 times in August.

Our view is Hold with a probability-weighted value of about $280, against the desk’s August figure of about $405. At $278.99, November 2 close, the value sits 0.4% above the price, inside our hold band. The whole cut is the multiple and the weights. What would change it is in section 5, and what the resting order in our own account did is in section 6.

1. Two tests passed. The cash test failed.

The operating quarter was strong by any ordinary measure. Revenue grew 25% to $127.6 billion, the thirteenth straight quarter of double-digit growth, and operating income grew 30% to $40.6 billion.[1] Google Cloud more than doubled: $30.7 billion of revenue, $11.1 billion of operating income, a 36.2% margin against 23.7% a year ago, with TPU system sales “a meaningful contributor” and the finance chief guiding to “a meaningful revenue ramp in 2027.”[1][3] The Cloud backlog, which we had set as the first of three proof tests before the report, came in at $560.8 billion in the 10-Q, against the $560 billion we required and $513.9 billion in June.[2][6][8] It cleared by less than one percent, and it cleared.

Search grew 12.0% to $63.4 billion, down from 17% in the second quarter. The chief business officer attributed most of the slowdown to currency, retail and media, and acknowledged that “the strong growth in AI Mode queries had an impact because of the lower monetization” on those queries, while calling most of them incremental.[3] Reported earnings of $2.12 a share were cut by $0.55 from an $8.7 billion mark-down of equity holdings, chiefly the SpaceX stake; before the mark, the figure was $2.67.[1] We underwrite the operating lines and treat the marks as noise in both directions, as we said in August when they ran the other way.

The third test was free cash flow turning positive with a path back to buybacks. Operating cash flow was $42.3 billion and capital expenditure was $51.4 billion, so free cash flow was negative $9.1 billion, after negative $5.9 billion in the second quarter.[1] Repurchases were zero for the third quarter running; $69.5 billion of the April 2025 authorization sits unused.[2] The finance chief’s explanation was the TPU business: “we build the inventory ahead of getting the revenue stream in.”[3] Inventory did rise, to $13.3 billion from $2.4 billion at year-end.[2] But the inventory build is $11 billion over nine months; the capital expenditure is $132 billion. The cash test failed on spending, and the spending is going up.

2. The funding turn, in the company’s own numbers

The August underwrite called the financing file the spine of the thesis: a company that returned about $13 billion a quarter had begun raising capital. The third quarter extended it. In the nine months to September, Alphabet issued $85.2 billion of debt net of costs and repaid $8.9 billion, sold $42.9 billion of common stock ($12.4 billion of it through the at-the-market program in the third quarter, with $27.6 billion of that program still available) and $19.1 billion of mandatory convertible preferred.[1][2] It bought back no stock. A year earlier the same nine months saw $39.8 billion of buybacks and $11.9 billion of net new debt.[1] Long-term debt stands at $125.1 billion, from $46.5 billion at year-end; the diluted share count is up 1.4% in a year, before the preferred converts in 2029.[1][2]

What the money is committed to is also on the record. Material purchase commitments reached $924.4 billion at September 30, from $811.0 billion in June; $213.4 billion is due within twelve months, and $802.1 billion is multi-year infrastructure, inventory, energy and content, mostly through 2031. Leases signed and not yet started add $130.5 billion. Property and equipment, net, is $372.8 billion against $246.6 billion at year-end, and depreciation grew 49% in the quarter.[1][2] The finance chief said the investment “will continue to weigh on the P&L in the form of higher depreciation expenses” and that third-party capacity coming online in the fourth quarter brings “margin headwinds in the near term.”[3]

The fourth quarter is already arithmetic. Nine-month capital expenditure was $132.0 billion; an unchanged $195–205 billion guide implies $63–73 billion in the fourth quarter, against operating cash flow that has run between $39 billion and $52 billion a quarter for five quarters.[1] Free cash flow will be negative again in the fourth quarter on the company’s own plan, and 2027 is guided higher from there. We stop treating that as a scenario. It is the path.

3. The August view, scored

First the record. On August 4 we underwrote Alphabet at Hold with a probability-weighted value of about $405: bull 30% at $450–480, base 45% at $390–420, bear 25% at $290–320, with buy gates at $335–340 and $318–325 and three proof tests for this report.[8] That piece was written for publication as the second note of the deployment series and never shipped. What readers saw was the series opener on July 31 with a wait-for-price alert at $318, two weekly notes in August that said the alert stood through the October report, and the August 14 essay recording that it never triggered.[7] Our coverage table has never carried the name. We score the August view here because we acted on it, and because an unpublished number scored honestly is worth more than a published one scored kindly. This is our first public rating on Alphabet.

From the August 4 anchor of $377.65 the shares are down 26.1% against the S&P 500 down 2.5%.[4] What the August view got right: it named the financing file as the thing the market grades, it refused to chase at 28 times earnings, it set the Microsoft-parity rule for the multiple, and two of its three proof tests read correctly. What it got wrong is the part that mattered. It priced the funding turn as a one-in-four bear case with a $290 floor, and its base case assumed funding would “normalize slowly.” The company instead spent 121% of its operating cash flow in a quarter and guided 2027 higher. The bear case was not a tail. It was the path, and the floor lasted one session.

The error in method. On August 18, with the stock at $344, we wrote that “the price came to the framework.” It had not. The price was falling because the funding leg was getting worse, and a price gate on a funding thesis, with no funding gate beside it, is a rule for buying a falling number. The thesis-break conditions below put a dollar threshold on the 2027 spending guide so that cannot happen twice.

4. The tree and the price

We take the Street’s 2027 earnings whole. Consensus is $15.17 a share on revenue of $628 billion, up 25%; the estimate has moved from $14.76 ninety days ago to $15.17 and has not yet absorbed this report.[5] On our arithmetic this year’s earnings are about $11 a share once the first-half investment gains and the third-quarter loss are stripped out, so the consensus asks for a 35% step in 2027. The bull and base cases accept it; the bear case cuts it to $13.50–14.00 as depreciation and third-party capacity land.

The multiple is the change. In August the base leg paid 26–28 times forward earnings. The rule we set then was that Alphabet should trade at or below Microsoft’s multiple until it funds itself again; Microsoft is at 22.1× its next fiscal year, which would be $335 for Alphabet, and Meta, the other buyer the market fines for spending, is at 17.9×.[5] Our base leg pays 18–20×, a 10–18% discount to Microsoft while the funding is external; the bull leg pays parity, 22–24×, if February brings a dated path back to positive free cash flow or a buyback; the bear leg pays 15–16×, where the shares bottomed in 2022 and in April 2025.

Two cross-checks. A discounted cash flow on the same path, with stock compensation treated as a cost, revenue growth fading from 25% to 4% by 2035, capital spending of $280 billion in each of 2027 and 2028 and then fading toward a tenth of revenue, and a 10.8% discount rate, gives a base value of $203 a share, $263 with stock compensation added back; the price equals the base path at a 9.2% discount rate. A reverse check on earnings says the price needs 8.7% annual earnings growth from the 2027 estimate at a 20× exit in 2031, or 11.6% at 18×. The two disagree the way they should on a business spending more than it generates: the earnings say the price is undemanding, the cash says it is at the top of what it supports. The tree sits between them.

Relative to the Street we are a long way below. The pre-report mean target was $430 across 54 analysts; the first post-report moves we have seen are to $300 from $350 at DA Davidson, which holds a Hold, to $400 from $425 at RBC and to $405 from $425 at Barclays.[9] We are consensus on the business and contrarian on the price.

5. What would change the rating

Our hold band is a twelve-to-eighteen-month probability-weighted value within roughly 10% below and 5% above the market price. At about $280, the view is Hold while the shares sit between about $267 and $311; a publication-day close above $311 would make the rating Reduce by the band, with no re-litigation, and we would say so. The next test is the fourth-quarter report, when management has said it will give the 2027 figure. Alphabet reported the December quarter on February 4 this year; the company has not announced a date, and we will take it from the company.

6. The order in our own account

An order to buy a small Alphabet position at $319.78, entered August 18 at the lower of the two August gates, had rested since then. It filled on Monday, October 26, when the shares traded as low as $316.60, three sessions before the report.[4] We sold the position before this note was published. We hold no shares in Alphabet at publication, and the disclosure below says so.

The lesson is the one in section 3: the order was placed at a price level, and the price level was reached because the thing we were worried about got worse. A resting order in a name whose thesis is about funding should have carried a funding condition. The thesis-break table above now does.
